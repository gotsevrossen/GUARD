High severity · Low confidenceGet help nowRepeated failed logins from one address

14 failed sign-ins to the file server in four minutes, all from 10.0.4.66.

Today, 11:07 AM▾

Get help now

Contact your IT provider or a security professional now.

This could be serious, and LightHouse can’t be sure what happened. Call your IT provider, a managed security provider or an incident-response firm, and tell them about this alert. While you wait:

  1. Don’t delete files, programs or logs. Whoever investigates will need them.
  2. Don’t reply to, contact or pay anyone demanding money.
  3. Write down what you saw: the time, anything on screen, and anything unusual.
  4. Disconnect the affected device from the network (unplug its cable or turn off its Wi-Fi) if that won’t stop critical work. Leave it switched on.

What this means

Fourteen sign-in attempts failed against one server inside four minutes, from an address that has never held a successful session. That rate and that pattern look like someone working through a list of passwords, not a person mistyping their own.

LightHouse’s suggestion — check with your IT provider first

  1. Block 10.0.4.66 first. Drop it at the firewall, or unplug the device if it sits on your own network. This stops the attempts while you look into it.
  2. Work out what the device is. It is not in your known-device list. Your router DHCP table will show the lease, and the switch will show which port it is on.
  3. Reset the password on any account it targeted and turn on lockout after five failed attempts, so a run like this stops itself next time.
  4. Check whether anything succeeded from that address before the failures began. If it did, treat that account as compromised.

Medium severity · Low confidenceUnusual DNS activity detected

A device made an unusual request. Review the device if this repeats.

Today, 10:42 AM▾

Needs a second opinion

LightHouse isn’t sure about this one.

The explanation below may be wrong, in either direction. Before you change anything, have someone technical (your IT provider or a tech-savvy colleague) look at this alert. Leave it open until they have.

What this means

front-desk-pc asked for 214 different subdomains of a single domain in three minutes. That shape of traffic is one way data gets smuggled out over DNS, but ad blockers, antivirus updaters and content networks produce it too. One burst on its own is usually harmless.

Recommended steps

  1. Find out who was on front-desk-pc at 10:42 and what they were doing. That often answers it outright.
  2. Run a malware scan on that device before you clear the alert.
  3. If the domain means nothing to you, block it at your DNS resolver and see whether anything on the machine breaks.
  4. Give it 24 hours. A single burst is noise; the same pattern returning daily is worth escalating.

Yesterday, 4:51 PM▾

What this means

Something checked a thousand doors on that machine to see which ones open. The firewall refused all of them, so nothing was reached. What matters now is where the scan came from.

Recommended steps

  1. Check whether the source was inside your network. An internal scanner usually means that device is already compromised and should come off the network today.
  2. If it came from outside, confirm no ports are forwarded to that PC from the internet. If none are, the firewall did its job and there is nothing further to do.
  3. Confirm the machine is patched, since a scan is often the step before an attempt.

Recommended steps

  3. Check the new expiry date reads a year out, then set a reminder for eleven months.
